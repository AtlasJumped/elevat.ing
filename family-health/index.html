Who this is for

This page is made for anybody who has a family member who is struggling with mental illness to a serious degree, and who is not getting the help they need from anywhere else.

We will be blunt about it. This site is for people who have got nowhere else to go.

Does any of this sound familiar?

Everything here is hypothetical. No names, no real people. But a lot of families will recognize it.

  • Your loved one is suspicious of everyone, makes accusations that are not true, and does not respect boundaries.
  • You asked for outside help, and you were told there is nothing anyone can do, because your loved one is not a danger to themselves or others.
  • Your loved one spent time in a hospital, came home, nothing had changed, and now they are angry at you.
  • Everyone in the family points at one person as "the sick one", and that person points right back.

That happens to a lot of people. And it can start to feel like there are no experts anywhere.

What this is

There is a theory out there called the holistic model of reality (HMR). As far as we know, it is the only theory that talks about the nature of reality from start to finish, including actionable insight for how humans should treat each other from a mental health perspective.

You are about to see our perspective on a very important topic for humanity. Every other website you see will tell you that it is not trying to give advice. The whole point of this site is different: we believe that we have found a better way to care for human beings than exists anywhere else.

"We are forging our own path, and we believe that we have better tools for handling human mental health than anybody else, based on this holistic model of reality that we have."

We encourage you to think hard and long about whether this tool is good and useful for you. And if it is, do something about it. Find a way to make other people benefit from this information.

Start with the model

The model: a narrative of mental health

Here is the baseline of the model. Here is what we believe.

1. Everything is a spectrum

Each part of a person exists on a spectrum, shaped like a bell curve. Most people sit in the middle, near the population average. Far to the left are the exemplars of society: people who are exceptionally healthy and capable at that trait. Far to the right are people who are extremely deficient at it, all the way out to people who do real harm to others.

So we did not create data points. We created spectrums, and you provide the data points on them, based on how you see your loved one. The model gives the framework; families give the data.

2. It is a well-being tool for anyone, not a sickness tracker

Most diagnoses today are a list of what is wrong with a person. There is no sense of what is good about them. Are they very intelligent? Are they witty? Are they funny? Those are signs of intelligence, and they matter to a person's mental state from a holistic perspective.

So this model rates healthy traits too. If you are healthy, you get a healthy score. If you are unhealthy, you get an unhealthy score. A vast majority of people land somewhere in the middle. And some people who are sure they are fine get to see that something they do is a bigger problem than they thought.

3. Everyone is on the spectrum, including the "healthy" ones

Medicine has a word for a person who does not recognize their own condition: anosognosia. We think everyone sits somewhere on that spectrum too. Families often point at one loved one and say "you are obviously sick", and the loved one points right back: "no, you have a problem too".

The people who think they are healthy can be one of the biggest problems in the mix. If you are deficient on one spectrum to a certain degree, for example a very high need to control, very high anxiety, or no sense of other people's boundaries, you can develop patterns that are just as hard on a family as the person everyone calls ill.

Ask yourself how good your own model of reality is. A loved one who has been told what to think and feel, for years, by people who were sometimes wrong, may have a valid reason for not hearing you out anymore.

4. Your framework of reality matters

It is hard to know your own place on these spectrums. Take religion as an example. Someone with a strong faith may hold that a behavior is fine when others think it is not. To someone with a different framework, they may seem off, even insane. Often it is just a different framework of reality, and the model has to account for that.

One of the most important measures of health is how you interact with society, and how open you are to its feedback. But openness to feedback also depends on how stable your own model of reality is compared with the people giving the feedback. And when society tries to control someone, that can push them further down the spectrum in a way nobody wants.

5. There are no experts. There are professionals.

Humanity relies on people we are never sure what to call: experts, or professionals. We want to be clear about it. When it comes to the human mind, there are no true experts yet. There are true professionals.

The problem is that families with a suffering loved one confuse a professional with an expert. They put too much trust in them. They see failures where there is not really failure, and they are not proactive enough. They trust the professional to own the problem, when the family needs to own it.

Many people in the system are good people with good intentions. But every one of them also sits somewhere on these spectrums, including understanding of reality and sense of self. And in modern mental health care, listening is a problem: visits are short, people get interrupted, and the family is rarely involved.

6. The family comes first

If somebody is doing fine and just wants to talk to someone, that is what therapists are good for, and that is great. But if somebody is really in trouble, we need a system that says the family comes first. The family owns this, if possible. The doctor's job at that point is to empower the family, and to do it while respecting the patient.

We do not want a society where people with unusual thoughts and behavior are unfairly controlled by people who do not like them. We believe force, meaning detainment or involuntary medication, belongs only where someone is critically ill, to the point of violence and direct harm. It is not okay to force it on a person who is simply annoying, even extremely annoying, even when you love them with all your heart and are sure medication would help.

7. Families need the right tools at the right time

Most people with a struggling loved one would be fine walking on eggshells now and then, as long as they walk on them at the right time and in the right place, and know where they can still be themselves. Many would happily say: "I would like this relationship to be 50/50, but I love this person, so I will accept 60/40. I know where I will give more, and where I will not."

And something almost nobody accounts for: in some cases, a family member has to move on. Families deserve the tools to fix the relationship, the time to try, and, from day one rather than when it is too late, an honest picture of what happens if it does not get better, including an exit plan.

Next: assess your loved one

Assess your loved one

Based on our model of reality, these are the things you need to know about your loved one. Each one is a spectrum. You provide the data point.

Everyone assesses everyone

If only one person gets assessed, that person will feel targeted. So every person in the family fills this in about every person in the family, including themselves. That way no one is singled out, and you may find patterns in yourself that everyone else already sees.

Next to every slider is "I prefer not to fill this one in". Press it and the slider grays out, no questions asked. The report lists it as a blind spot. A family can always truthfully say it completed the assessment, 100%, for every member.

Private by design: your answers stay in this browser on this device. Nothing is sent anywhere. Use nicknames, not real names.

The solution

The solution is not for you to look at our website. The solution is for everybody to learn the model, and for you to take this website and give it to your mental health care practitioner.

  1. Learn the model. Read the model together as a family. Talk about where you agree and where you do not.
  2. Everyone assesses everyone. Each person fills in the assessment about each person, themselves included. Compare your reports.
  3. Give this site to your practitioner. Share the link and your reports with the professional who works with your loved one.
  4. Follow the family-involvement protocol below.

The family-involvement protocol

Instead of every practitioner inventing this wheel from scratch and learning it slowly, here is the packet that anyone can use.

When

The moment the practitioner decides a person is on the right side of the spectrum: no longer operating in the mostly healthy zone, now operating in the mostly unhealthy zone.

The conversation

"You are no longer in the mostly healthy zone. I would like to talk to you about how we are going to include your family." This is a tough conversation, and professionals need to be trained to navigate it. This is where a professional becomes an expert.

Buy-in

From the first sessions, the goal is to earn as much buy-in from the patient as possible, so information can be shared with the family as soon as possible. Some people will say "no problem". Some will push back, and the professional has to navigate that with care.

One coach on both sides

The person coaching the patient should be the same person coaching the family. Families with a loved one in care can also ask that practitioner directly: "What are your protocols for families? How would you teach me to care for someone like this?"

Family intake

Once the family is included, a well-run intake meeting, then care coordinated across the whole family, not just one person.

Honest outlook

Realistic, truthful updates: "They love you, they are willing, I have high hopes", or "I have seen this before, and this path is hard. Here is what you can do." Tools, time to try, and an exit plan known from day one.

"We can come up with a healthcare protocol that elevates the entire family."

Everyone assessing everyone, so no one feels targeted: that is not engineered into current healthcare. It is engineered into ours.

Human trials

This button is here because we have architected, from start to finish, a human trial that we believe is important for humanity.

The hypothesis

Some people in a mental health crisis, during mania for example, talk at length about people and moments from their past that seem unimportant to others but clearly matter to them, and jump between ideas that seem disjointed. Our hypothesis, one we are not married to, is that if a person's own words are recorded over weeks or years, AI could find patterns in them that are actually relevant to their care, and could give their professional real talking points.

As far as we know, psychiatrists are not recommending this today. It is a hypothesis. There is an avenue here, even if not a solution right now.

The trial, in brief

  • Who: people who agree to take part, with informed consent, and professionals trained in this model.
  • What: with consent, the participant's own speech is recorded and transcribed. AI looks for recurring themes and gives the professional talking points.
  • Measure: whether those talking points help care, as judged by the participant, the family and the professional.
  • Safeguards: consent that can be withdrawn at any time, strict privacy for every recording, and review by an ethics board before anything starts.
